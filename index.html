The discovery

The gut-skin theory that dermatologists ignored for nearly a century

In 1930, two dermatologists published a paper with an idea that was decades ahead of its moment. Dr. John Stokes and Dr. Donald Pillsbury proposed that what happens inside your gut can directly drive what shows up on your skin.

They believed that stress changed gut bacteria, increased intestinal permeability, and triggered the kind of systemic inflammation that shows up as breakouts, redness, and irritation on the skin. Their recommended remedy included Lactobacillus acidophilus cultures — the same strain now in BushAid.

Stokes & Pillsbury, 1930

The gut and the skin are connected through a system of shared inflammation — and when one is out of balance, the other will show it.

Paraphrased from the original 1930 clinical paper, later reviewed in Gut Pathogens (Bowe & Logan, 2011)

For 80 years the paper was ignored. Then in 2011, researchers Bowe and Logan revived the work — finding that modern research had quietly validated many of its core ideas. Today, researchers call this the gut-skin axis. Most probiotics on shelves still haven't made the trip.

The real reason

Why your last probiotic probably never reached your skin

Most probiotics were designed with one job: digestion. Bloating, regularity, gut comfort. And for that narrow job, many work reasonably well — for a while.

But the bacteria in a typical probiotic behave more like houseguests than residents. They help while they're around. When you stop or the routine breaks, they leave. That's why so many people feel better for a week or two and then drift back.

If any of these sound familiar, this is probably why:

  • "I took it for a month and felt nothing."
  • "It worked for the first week, then stopped."
  • "My digestion improved but my skin stayed the same."
  • "I've tried two different brands and I still bloat."
  • "My doctor said it probably wouldn't do much — and they were right."

None of these are your fault. Research has also found that people with frequent breakouts tend to have lower zinc levels — and the vast majority of probiotics contain no zinc at all.
