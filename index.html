The discovery

The gut-skin theory that dermatologists ignored for nearly a century

In 1930, two dermatologists working at a time when medicine was just beginning to understand the gut, published a paper with an idea that was decades ahead of its moment. Dr. John Stokes and Dr. Donald Pillsbury proposed something that would seem obvious today: that what happens inside your gut can directly drive what shows up on your skin.

They believed that emotional stress changed gut bacteria, increased what we now call intestinal permeability, and triggered the kind of systemic inflammation that shows up as breakouts, redness, and irritation on the skin. Their recommended remedy included Lactobacillus acidophilus cultures — the same strain now in BushAid.

Stokes & Pillsbury, 1930

The gut and the skin are connected through a system of shared inflammation — and when one is out of balance, the other will show it.

Paraphrased from the original 1930 clinical paper, later reviewed in Gut Pathogens (Bowe & Logan, 2011)

The paper was largely ignored. For the next 80 years, dermatology and gut health were treated as two completely separate fields. Probiotics, when they finally became mainstream, were built almost entirely around digestion — bloating relief, regularity, bowel comfort. The skin was never part of the formula.

Then, in 2011, researchers Bowe and Logan published a review in Gut Pathogens that revived Stokes and Pillsbury's work. They found that many aspects of the original theory had been quietly validated by modern research — gut bacteria influencing systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and the skin's behavior. Today, researchers call this the gut-skin axis.

It took 80 years to get back to an idea that two doctors had in 1930. And most probiotics on shelves today still haven't made the trip.

The real reason

Why your last probiotic probably never reached your skin

Most probiotics were designed with one job: digestion. Bloating, regularity, gut comfort. That's it. And for that narrow job, many of them work reasonably well — for a while.

But here's what they almost never tell you: the bacteria in a typical probiotic behave more like houseguests than residents. They help while they're around. When you stop taking the capsule, or when the routine breaks, they leave. That's why so many people feel better for a week or two and then drift back to where they started.

If any of these sound familiar, this is probably why:

  • "I took it for a month and felt nothing."
  • "It worked for the first week, then stopped."
  • "My digestion improved but my skin stayed exactly the same."
  • "I've tried two different brands and I still bloat at every meal."
  • "My doctor said it probably wouldn't do much — and they were right."

None of these are your fault. They're what happens when a digestion-only formula meets a problem that runs across three systems at once: your gut, your skin, and your immune response. Treating one side of that connection and leaving the other two untouched isn't a failure of probiotics. It's a failure of how most probiotics are designed.

There's also something else most formulas leave out entirely. Research has found that people with frequent breakouts tend to have lower zinc levels. And the vast majority of probiotics — including the premium ones — contain no zinc at all. Your gut-skin connection has a missing piece, and it isn't a higher CFU count.
